# REWRITE Core Framework

Read this reference when applying the full reasoning loop, deciding where a statement belongs, handling anomalies, or deciding when to stop.

## The Seven Moves

### R — Research Question

Do not begin from a preferred method, model, dataset, benchmark, or visualization. State what the study must learn. Open a question space through only the dimensions relevant to the phenomenon:

- **Whether:** Does the effect exist, remain after controls, replicate, or generalize?
- **What:** Which component, feature, or variable drives it?
- **How:** Through what process or pathway does it occur?
- **Why:** Which mechanism or explanation accounts for it?
- **When:** Under which conditions does it appear or fail?
- **Where:** In which domains, tasks, populations, organisms, systems, or regimes does it hold?
- **To what extent:** How large is the effect and where is its quantitative boundary?

The research start should connect:

> Scientific Question → Hypothesis → Experimental Design

### E — Examine the Literature

Use literature as coordinates for novelty and interpretation, not as decoration.

Before the study, determine whether the question has been answered, which explanations and competing hypotheses exist, what boundary conditions are known, and what this work could add.

After an important finding, position it as one or more of:

- **Confirm:** supports an existing conclusion;
- **Contradict:** conflicts with an existing conclusion;
- **Refine:** adds conditions, boundaries, or a more precise explanation;
- **Extend:** carries a conclusion into a new setting;
- **Reframe:** changes how the problem itself should be understood.

The useful question is not merely “Which prior paper agrees?” but “What knowledge does this finding change, delimit, or extend?”

### W — Work / Experiment

Every experiment should answer a named question. Do not recommend an ablation, embedding visualization, case study, or benchmark only because papers conventionally contain one.

### R — Read the Finding

Keep these levels distinct:

- **Data:** direct observations, measurements, comparisons, or estimates.
- **Finding:** the qualitative statement directly supported by those data.
- **1-hop implication:** the nearest defensible meaning of that finding.

A Results evidence unit should follow:

> Question → Experiment → Data → Finding → 1-hop Implication

Do not leap from one result directly to a field-wide principle.

### I — Interrogate the Finding

Ask why, how, what, whether, when, where, and to what extent only where they expose a meaningful mechanism, alternative explanation, boundary, or decision.

### T — Test Answerability

For each important new question:

- If the current data or a feasible added experiment can answer it, design the analysis or experiment and make the answer a new Results unit.
- If the present study cannot answer it, explain the blocking scope or design constraint; only then derive a limitation and future study.
- If it undermines the validity of the main comparison or makes the claim unsupported, treat it as a redesign or claim-narrowing issue.

### E — Extend or Exit

Extend the current project only when the question is important and answerable within scope. Otherwise exit the loop deliberately by recording the unresolved question, the actual limitation, and the future experiment.

## Anomaly Branch

After each important experiment, ask whether the result matches the expectation.

1. **Possible technical error:** check preprocessing, implementation, measurement, leakage, batch effects, contamination, and statistical artifacts. Correct and rerun when warranted.
2. **Possible noise:** test reproducibility. Do not elevate a non-reproducible event into a main finding.
3. **Stable anomaly:** treat it as evidence that the hypothesis or question may be incomplete. Use:

> Unexpected Finding → Rewrite the Question

Do not conceal or force a reproducible anomaly into the original narrative.

## The Two Loops

The **inner loop** grows the current project:

> Finding → Question → Answerability → Experiment → Finding

The **outer loop** deepens understanding:

> Finding → Literature → Interpretation → Principle → New Question

Use both loops to decide what belongs in new Results and what belongs in Discussion.

## Results and Discussion Placement

- Direct data, comparisons, and observations → **Results**
- Meaning of one concrete result → **Results subsection ending: 1-hop implication**
- Integrated meaning of multiple findings for this study → **Discussion opening: 2-hop interpretation**
- Mechanism or principle beyond this study → **Discussion middle: tentative general principle**
- Unresolved questions generated by findings or principle → **Late Discussion**
- Why the current study cannot answer an important question → **Limitations**
- Experiment designed to answer that question → **Future Studies**

Results should be organized around scientific answers, not technical operations. A subsection title should normally say what was learned.

## Limitations and Future Studies

A limitation is not simply something the study did not do. It is a constraint that prevents the study from answering an important question generated by its own findings.

Use the chain:

> Finding → New Question → Why Current Study Cannot Answer → Limitation → Future Study

Each future study should be an experiment or analysis that addresses a named unresolved question, not an unrelated wishlist item.

## Reviewer Stress Test

Generate the three most consequential expert-reviewer challenges and classify each:

- **A — Added experiment can resolve it:** return to Results.
- **B — Existing data or clearer reasoning can resolve it:** strengthen Results or Discussion.
- **C — Current scope cannot resolve it:** state the limitation and corresponding future study.
- **D — Fatal flaw:** redesign the comparison or narrow the central claim.

Reviewer criticism is a stress test; it is not automatically a limitation.

## Deadline Mode and Stop Rule

Rank work in this order:

1. questions whose answer could overturn or materially narrow the central claim;
2. likely fatal reviewer challenges such as leakage, memorization, unfair baselines, missing key controls, or viable alternative explanations;
3. low-cost analyses or controls with high explanatory value;
4. important boundary conditions;
5. work that belongs to a separate study.

The project may stop expanding Results when:

1. the central scientific question has a credible answer;
2. key competing explanations have been excluded to a reasonable degree;
3. major reviewer challenges have been handled;
4. the most important boundary conditions have basic evidence;
5. remaining questions require work substantially beyond the current scope.

The target is a minimum complete, credible, and defensible scientific story—not answers to every possible question.
